The overachiever had a suit on, someone had fingerless gloves, and she had her coat. It was the way of things in that class.

It was a grey thing, with fur so dense you could not see the bottom, and black dots like overeager freckles. It had a sheen to it, some kind of fabric softener, perhaps, and looked healthier than her salt-brined hair.

It was not the first time Walter had seen her. She swam in the bay without her coat or anything else. Walter begged his coach until she allowed him to practice his figure skating on the lake ice.

There was a zipper, and she had put on a hood and drawstrings herself.

She packed a bagel from home, with cucumber and tuna salad. He brought her a coffee, her usual order.

"Hi."

She closed her notebook.

Her name was Siobhan; she was from Newfoundland. Her father kept a lighthouse, and she was brought up in a boarding school.

Her mother had died when she was young, and the father didn't call.

---

She hung her coat on the chair inside out, between her and the back of her seat.

"A rum highball for me," she said to the waiter. "And the sablefish."

He asked for a dynamite roll. She had a laugh like a seagull.

They talked about school: she was failing Organic Chemistry, and he had an insistence on bringing a pot of ink to exams.

He paid the bill when she went to the powder room; she had taken her coat with her.

---

She picked up skating from him, and never progressed past braking by way of a wall. He spent more time off the rink, because she was getting jealous of how well he could spin. There were breakfasts at two in the morning, and pirouettes in parking lots.

In the summer, he swam faster than her. In the fall and after, she won by default.

The dorm room had a narrow twin bed, and there was nowhere for Siobhan that was not next to him.

"Does it not get suffocating under the coat?"

"Nah."

By Thanksgiving, she let him hold her under the coat.

---

A storm came through and thundered until the lights went out. She pretended to be half asleep, with her back to him, and the coat between them.

"Walter?" She was shaking.

"Yes?"

"Never let me go."

"Never."

---

Christmas was at grandma's, in a house that had always been the family's. There was a pier out front that Walter never saw with a boat.

Grandma Sigrid was a woman of eighty-five, and had stopped pretending to be decent five years prior.

Siobhan was made to help Grandma Sigrid in the kitchen, reaching for cupboards and peeling potatoes. Grandma chased him out of the kitchen.

"Siobhan, dear."

"Yes, Madam?"

"How are your feet?"

"Good, Madam."

"You will want to ask him to put in carpets. My mother did the same."

Siobhan was silent.

"She left the door open when my father woke up; breakfast was still warm on the table."

"Did she ever come back?"

"No. The sea keeps."

Grandma Sigrid made Siobhan grab baking powder.

"She never let my father help."

That night, the coat was on his chair, where he could see it.

"What did Grandma say?"

"Nothing," Siobhan said. "We talked about carpets."

---

He graduated first, and they found an attic suite close to campus. Laundry was in the basement, coin operated.

She was busy with her thesis, and it became his job to do the washing. The coat needed its own cycle, with no fabric softener. It lived in the wardrobe, next to his winter things, a toolbox, and the safe they got to put the deed in.

There was a month, when he was away for a competition, when laundry was not done at all. She had the clothes to go through it, and the wardrobe only turned half-empty.

She took out her coat. She hung it up on the wardrobe door and looked at it until dawn.

He got a bronze, and brought back tinned sardines.

---

They had a quiet year just to themselves. Parks, games, and a few trips up the coast or inland. She stole his hoodie and insisted that it was always hers, and he learned her favorite brand of insoles.

She insisted on hiking, even though she would get dizzy just at the foothills. They camped half a day's hike away from the parking lot.

"There is a tattoo on your ankle."

"You never noticed?"

"I just did. Is that a knot?"

"A sheet bend, from a textbook."

Walter brushed his thumb over it.

"I got it after last Christmas."

---

Then came their third fall. The radio said the atmospheric river was going to be worse that year. Trees were knocked down in the park downstairs, and power went out on the second day.

The road outside had turned to a river, and the drains were clogged by dry leaves.

She couldn't sleep to the sound of rain. The thunder made it worse. He made hot cocoa with milk, and then milk ran out three days in, and he made it with water.

She drank it, looking out the westward window. Not at the building it looked out to, but the water you couldn't see from there.

"Walter?"

"Yes?"

"My coat. I need you to lock it up."

He tried to bring her back to the couch, but she said, "No, now, please. I need you to."

They went to the wardrobe, with the toolbox and the grey coat with black freckles. She held the coat, and brushed at the fur according to its grain. It did not have a heartbeat, but the fur still stood back up where her finger had just been.

They were kneeling now, before the open safe and its king and queen, their passports.

"A day like this. I will want it back."

"You will."

She tucked the right shoulder under the left, and folded it once horizontally.

"And I will say horrible things to get it."

"You are too kind to."

"No, I will." She placed her coat in the safe.

"Okay."

She was shaking as she pulled her hands back. Walter brought her head into his chest.

"Hey, hey, it's okay. We don't have to."

"Please," Siobhan sobbed. "I want to stay. I want to stay so much. Say you won't ever give it back."

"I won't ever give it back, not even if the sky was falling, no siree."

He held her head with one hand, and closed the safe with the other. He carried her back to the couch, and covered her with the hoodie that was always hers.

He set a new combination, and when he walked back to the living room she was already asleep on the chaise.

He sat next to her, and the overcast light was the only thing that woke them in the morning.

---

There is a tub of Legos on top of the safe now, and his briefcase leaning against the front. The carpet ends at the wardrobe's accordion doors.

Their older one asked what was under the Legos.

"Paperwork, some of my old medals, and your mother's old coat."

The child asked why not sell a coat nobody was wearing.

He looked at his wife, settling the accounts through her glasses.

"Because it reminds her of home."
